# Model Card: Clause Classifier

## Summary

A multi-label text classifier that identifies which of 19 legal clause
categories a sentence belongs to, replacing an earlier keyword-matching
heuristic. Trained on real, expert-annotated contracts — not synthetic
or self-generated labels.

## Training data

[CUAD](https://www.atticusprojectai.org/cuad) (Contract Understanding
Atticus Dataset) — 510 real commercial contracts, expert-annotated across
41 clause categories in a SQuAD-style extractive QA format.

19 categories were selected for training: substantive, risk-relevant
clauses with at least 100 example contracts each. Pure metadata fields
(Document Name, Parties, Agreement Date, ...) were excluded since they
aren't clauses and are already covered by named-entity extraction.

## Method

1. Each contract is split into sentences (spaCy sentencizer).
2. A sentence is labeled positive for a category if it overlaps that
   category's answer span in the CUAD annotations (multi-label — a
   sentence can belong to multiple categories).
3. Sentences are embedded with a frozen `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`
   sentence-transformer (no fine-tuning — this keeps training CPU-feasible;
   see "Why not fine-tune a transformer" below).
4. A One-vs-Rest logistic regression is trained on top of the embeddings.
5. Negative (no-label) sentences are subsampled 3:1 against positives per
   split, since the raw dataset is >90% negative and most of that carries
   little training signal.

Train/test split is done **by contract**, not by sentence, so no
document's own sentences appear in both sets.

## Metrics

Evaluated on a held-out 20% of contracts. Full per-category breakdown
across four decision thresholds is in
`models/clause_classifier/eval_report.txt` (generated by
`training/train_clause_model.py`, not checked in — regenerate it by
running the script).

At the production threshold (0.75):

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Precision (micro-avg) | 0.38 |
| Recall (micro-avg) | 0.77 |
| F1 (micro-avg) | 0.51 |

Strongest categories: Governing Law (F1 0.89), Insurance (0.91).
Weakest: Exclusivity, Change Of Control, Non-Compete — these recall most
real instances but also produce more false positives.

The threshold (0.75, up from the default 0.5) was chosen by sweeping
{0.5, 0.65, 0.75, 0.85} and picking the point that roughly doubles
precision over the default while keeping recall high — appropriate for a
screening tool where missing a real clause is worse than an extra
false-positive flag for a human to dismiss.

## Known limitations

- **Negation handling.** The model sometimes flags both "Cap On
  Liability" and "Uncapped Liability" for the same clause because it
  keys off the word "liability" without reliably parsing negation
  ("shall NOT be capped"). A post-processing rule keeps only the
  higher-confidence label when this pair collides — a mitigation, not a
  fix at the model level.
- **Superficial pattern matching.** Found during testing: a sentence
  about an employee's time commitment ("devote 80% of his time...") gets
  misclassified as "Revenue/Profit Sharing" — the model is pattern-matching
  on numeric/percentage phrasing, not real semantic understanding of
  financial terms.
- **No out-of-domain detection.** The pipeline will "analyze" any PDF,
  including non-contracts (tested against a resume) — it has no concept
  of "this isn't a contract" and will still return entities, clause
  guesses, and a risk score.
- **Missed clauses.** A synthetic lease agreement with an explicit
  Governing Law clause wasn't detected above threshold — recall isn't
  100% even for well-represented categories.

This is a screening aid, not a substitute for legal review — true of any
automated contract analysis tool.

## Why the production classifier isn't a fine-tuned transformer

The original CUAD paper fine-tunes RoBERTa/DeBERTa for extractive QA,
which needs a GPU and hours of training. `notebooks/bert-training.ipynb`
is earlier exploratory work — loading BERT and the real CUAD dataset via
HuggingFace `transformers`/`datasets` and inspecting CUAD's structure —
but it never combined the two into a real training run (its
"training" cells fit on 4 hardcoded example sentences, not CUAD).

`training/train_bert_classifier.py` picks that up for real: it fine-tunes
`distilbert-base-uncased` on the same CUAD sentence data as the
production classifier, for a direct comparison.

## Fine-tuned transformer comparison

`training/train_bert_classifier.py` fine-tuned `distilbert-base-uncased`
for 3 epochs on 6,000 training sentences (capped down from the full
22,204 available -- see below for why), evaluated on 1,500 held-out
test sentences. Took 89 minutes on CPU. Full report:
`models/bert_clause_classifier/eval_report.txt` (not checked in --
regenerate by running the script).

At its best threshold (0.3):

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Precision (micro-avg) | 0.76 |
| Recall (micro-avg) | 0.42 |
| F1 (micro-avg) | 0.54 |

That F1 is *higher* than the production model's 0.51 -- but the
headline number is misleading. On categories with enough training
examples, DistilBERT clearly wins: Governing Law (F1 0.96 vs 0.89),
Insurance (0.91 vs 0.91), Anti-Assignment (0.73 vs 0.50). But **11 of
the 19 categories score exactly 0.00 precision/recall/F1** --
Termination For Convenience, Post-Termination Services, Exclusivity,
Renewal Term, Minimum Commitment, Non-Transferable License, Ip
Ownership Assignment, Change Of Control, Non-Compete, Notice Period To
Terminate Renewal, and Covenant Not To Sue. The model never predicts
these categories at all.

This is a direct consequence of the training set being capped at 6,000
sentences to fit a CPU training budget: categories that were already
less common become too sparse to learn from at that scale. The
production model, trained on the full 22,204-sentence set, has *some*
signal for every category (its worst F1 is 0.20, not 0.00).

**This is why the production pipeline uses the frozen-embedding
approach, not this fine-tuned model**: better coverage across all 19
categories beats higher peak performance on a handful of them for a
tool meant to screen a whole contract. Given a GPU (removing the
CPU-imposed training-set cap), fine-tuning on the full dataset would
likely beat the production model outright -- the failures here are a
training-budget artifact, not a ceiling on what fine-tuning can do.
